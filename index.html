Lesson 1

Skills you need first

Exponents and radicals are built on six small skills. Most mistakes in this topic are really mistakes in one of these.

1.1 Integers and signs

When you multiply signed numbers: same signs give a positive, different signs give a negative. With exponents this means a negative base raised to an even power is positive, and to an odd power is negative.

(−2)2 = 4even power → positive
(−2)3 = −8odd power → negative
(−1)100 = 1even power → positive

Parentheses decide what the base is. Watch the difference:

1.2 Order of operations (GEMDAS)

Exponents come before multiplication, division, addition and subtraction. That is why −32 = −9: the exponent is done before the negative sign is applied.

  1. GGrouping symbols: ( ), [ ], fraction bars, radical signs
  2. EExponents and roots
  3. MDMultiplication and division, left to right
  4. ASAddition and subtraction, left to right

Example: 2 + 3 · 42 = 2 + 3 · 16 = 2 + 48 = 50 (not 202).

1.3 Divisibility rules

These let you find factors quickly when breaking numbers apart.

1.4 Prime factorization (the ladder method)

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …). Prime factorization writes a number as a product of primes. This is the single most important skill for simplifying radicals.

The ladder: divide by the smallest prime that works, write the quotient below, and repeat until you reach 1. The primes on the left are the answer.

1.5 Perfect squares and perfect cubes

A perfect square is a whole number times itself; a perfect cube is a whole number used as a factor three times. Knowing these by heart makes simplifying radicals much faster. Tap a card to check yourself.

1.6 Fractions: multiply and cancel

Multiply straight across, then cancel common factors. Breaking numbers into primes first makes cancelling easy. The same idea is behind the quotient rule for exponents.

Quick checks

What is (−5)2? And −52?

(−5)2 = 25 because the base is −5. −52 = −25 because only 5 is squared.

Write 84 as a product of primes.

84 = 2 · 2 · 3 · 7 = 22 · 3 · 7

Is 1 a prime number?

No. A prime has exactly two different factors. 1 has only one factor.

Lesson 2

The laws of exponents

Every law below comes from one idea: an exponent counts how many times a base is used as a factor. Watch each law get built from that idea, so you never have to memorize it blindly.

2.1 What an exponent means

53
base: the number being multiplied exponent: how many times it is a factor

Read 53 as “5 to the third power” or “5 cubed.” It means 5 · 5 · 5 = 125, not 5 · 3.

2.2 Product rule

Product ruleam · an = am + nSame base, multiplying → keep the base, add the exponents.

2.3 Quotient rule

Quotient ruleam ÷ an = am − n, a ≠ 0Same base, dividing → keep the base, subtract the exponents (top minus bottom).

2.4 Power of a power

Power of a power(am)n = am · nA power raised to a power → multiply the exponents.

2.5 Power of a product

Power of a product(ab)n = anbnThe exponent goes to every factor inside the parentheses, numbers included.

2.6 Power of a quotient

Power of a quotient(a/b)n = an/bn, b ≠ 0The exponent goes to the numerator and the denominator.

2.7 Zero exponent

Zero exponenta0 = 1, a ≠ 0Any nonzero base to the zero power is 1. Not 0!

Why? Follow the pattern. Each time the exponent goes down by 1, the value is divided by the base.

2.8 Negative exponent

Negative exponenta−n = 1/an and 1/a−n = anA negative exponent means reciprocal. Move the factor across the fraction bar and the exponent becomes positive.

2.9 All the laws on one table

Quick checks

Simplify a7 · a5

a12. Same base, multiplying: add 7 + 5.

Simplify (4m3)2

42(m3)2 = 16m6. Don't forget to square the 4.

Evaluate 3−2

1/32 = 1/9. The answer is positive.

Evaluate −80

−1. Only 8 is raised to the 0 power: −(80) = −(1) = −1.

Lesson 3

Simplifying expressions with exponents

Real problems use several laws at once. Follow the same order every time and you won't get lost.

3.1 The simplification process

  1. Clear parentheses using the power-of-a-product, power-of-a-quotient and power-of-a-power rules.
  2. Group like bases: numbers with numbers, x’s with x’s, y’s with y’s.
  3. Combine each group: multiply the numbers, add exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing.
  4. Rewrite negative exponents as positive by moving those factors across the fraction bar.
  5. Check: each base appears once, no negative or zero exponents remain, and number fractions are reduced.

3.2 Mistakes to avoid

x3 · x4 = x12x3 · x4 = x7

Multiplying powers means adding exponents. Multiply only for a power of a power.

23 · 22 = 4523 · 22 = 25

Keep the base. Do not multiply the bases.

x2 · y3 = (xy)5x2y3

Different bases cannot be combined.

(3x)2 = 3x2(3x)2 = 9x2

The exponent goes to every factor in the parentheses.

(x + y)2 = x2 + y2(x + y)2 = x2 + 2xy + y2

The power rules work for products, not sums.

2x−1 = 1/(2x)2x−1 = 2/x

The negative exponent belongs only to x. The 2 stays on top.

Quick checks

Simplify (2a3)3 · a−4

8a9 · a−4 = 8a5

Simplify 15x2y ÷ 5x6y4

3x−4y−3 = 3/(x4y3)

Lesson 4

What a radical means

A root undoes a power. If 72 = 49, then the square root of 49 is 7.

4.1 Parts of a radical

index: which root (no index means 2) radical sign radicand: the number inside

∛64 = 4 because 43 = 64. Read it as “the cube root of 64.”

4.2 A square root is the side of a square

The square root of a number is the side length of a square with that area. That's where the name comes from.

Not every number makes a perfect square. 12 tiles can't form a square, so √12 is not a whole number (it's about 3.46). That's why we simplify radicals instead of evaluating them.

4.3 Even and odd index

√25 = 5principal (positive) root
√−25 is not realeven index, negative radicand
∛−8 = −2odd index: negatives are fine

No real number squared gives a negative, so an even root of a negative number is not a real number. An odd root of a negative number is negative, because (−2)3 = −8.

4.4 Radicals as rational exponents

Rational exponentam/n = (n√a)m = n√(am)“Power over root.” The denominator is the index, the numerator is the power.

So √x = x1/2 and ∛x = x1/3. All the laws of exponents still work with fraction exponents.

Lesson 5

Simplifying radicals

A radical is in simplest form when the radicand has no factor that is a perfect power of the index, there is no fraction inside the radical, and there is no radical in a denominator.

5.1 The breakdown method (prime factors)

Product propertyn√(ab) = n√a · n√bThat's why a group of n equal factors can leave the radical as one factor.
  1. Break the radicand into prime factors (use the ladder). Write each variable power as repeated factors.
  2. Group identical factors into groups the size of the index: pairs for √, triples for ∛.
  3. Pull out one factor from each group. Leftover factors stay inside.
  4. Multiply everything outside together and everything inside together.

Variables are assumed to be non-negative so that, for example, √x² = x.

5.2 The shortcut: largest perfect-square factor

Once you know your perfect squares, you can skip the full factorization. Find the largest perfect square that divides the radicand.

If you pick a perfect square that isn't the largest, you still get there, but you have to simplify again:

Quick checks

Simplify √50

√(25 · 2) = 5√2

Simplify ∛40

∛(8 · 5) = 2∛5

Simplify √(48x3)

48 = 2·2·2·2·3 and x3 = x·x·x. Two pairs of 2’s and one pair of x’s come out: 4x√(3x)

Lesson 6

Operations on radicals

Add, subtract, multiply and divide radicals, then write the answer in simplest form.

6.1 Adding and subtracting: like radicals only

Like radicals have the same index and the same radicand. Combine them the way you combine like terms: 2a + 5a = 7a, so 2√3 + 5√3 = 7√3.

Radicals that look unlike may become like radicals after simplifying. Always simplify first.

√2 + √3 is not √5. Unlike radicals cannot be combined.

6.2 Multiplying

Multiplya√b · c√d = ac√(bd)Same index → multiply outside by outside, inside by inside, then simplify.

6.3 Dividing

Quotient property√a ÷ √b = √(a/b), b > 0Divide the radicands under one radical, then simplify.

6.4 Rationalizing the denominator

Simplest form has no radical in the denominator. Multiply by a form of 1 that turns the denominator into a whole number.

When the denominator is a binomial like 3 − √5, multiply by its conjugate 3 + √5. Because (a − b)(a + b) = a2 − b2, the radical disappears.

Quick checks

Simplify 3√8 − √18

3 · 2√2 − 3√2 = 6√2 − 3√2 = 3√2

Simplify 2√3 · 5√6

10√18 = 10 · 3√2 = 30√2

Rationalize 5/√5

5√5 / 5 = √5
