Before the fun part

Four tools in your pocket

Almost every mistake in this topic is really a mistake with one of these. Quick pass, then we go.

Tool 1 · Signs: who gets squared?

Are (−3)² and −3² the same number?

Aha

Parentheses decide the base. With them, −3 is squared. Without them, only the 3 is, and the minus waits outside. Same idea: an even power of a negative is positive, an odd power is negative.

Tool 2 · Primes: every number is built from bricks

Primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11…) are the bricks. The ladder takes any number apart: divide by the smallest prime that fits, repeat until you hit 1. Break a number and watch.

Aha

Every number has exactly one set of prime bricks. 360 is always 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3 · 5. Try 97: it won't break, because it is a brick.

Tool 3 · Squares and cubes: spot them on sight

Radicals get fast when you recognize these. Tap a card, say the answer out loud first, then flip it.

Tool 4 · Order of operations: exponents jump the queue

  1. GGrouping: ( ), fraction bars, radical signs

  3. MDMultiply / divide, left to right
  4. ASAdd / subtract, left to right

Exponents

Why the rules work

There's only one idea here. Once you see it, all seven rules become things you could have figured out yourself.

The one idea: an exponent counts the multiplications

Start with 1. The exponent says how many times to multiply by the base. Drag the slider and watch what happens when it reaches zero, then goes below.

Aha

Zero means "multiplied zero times", so you still just have the 1 you started with. Negative means you divide instead of multiply, so you land on fractions. Nobody made those up. They're what the pattern does when you keep going.

Multiplying powers

What is x3 · x2?

Aha

You're not multiplying exponents, you're counting x's. Three x's and two more x's make five. That is why you add: am · an = am+n

Dividing powers

What is x5 ÷ x2?

Aha

Division is cancelling. Each x on the bottom knocks out one x on top. Whatever survives is the difference: am ÷ an = am−n

A power of a power

What is (x2)3?

Aha

The outside exponent says how many copies of the inside. Three copies of "two x's" is six x's. Copies of copies means multiply: (am)n = amn

The exponent is contagious

(2x)3 equals…

Aha

An exponent outside parentheses infects every factor inside, numbers included. (ab)n = anbn and the same for fractions, top and bottom.

Zero: the weird one

What is 50?

Aha

Anything divided by itself is 1. The quotient rule says the same thing is x3−3 = x0. Both are true, so x0 = 1. (And yes, you saw it in the slider.)

Negative: it flips, it doesn't go negative

What is 2−3?

Aha

A negative exponent just moves the factor across the fraction bar and the exponent turns positive. Down becomes up, up becomes down: a−n = 1/an

Put the pieces together

Messy expressions are just laundry. Sort it into piles: numbers in one, each letter in its own. Then deal with one pile at a time.

The laundry method

  1. Open the parentheses. The outside exponent infects everything inside.
  2. Sort the piles: numbers, x’s, y’s…
  3. Handle each pile: add exponents when multiplying, subtract when dividing.
  4. Flip the negatives to the other side of the bar.

In 12a5b2 ÷ 4a2b6, where do the b’s end up?

Aha

Whichever side has more of a letter keeps the leftovers. A negative exponent is just the sign that the leftovers are on the bottom.

Radicals

Roots undo powers

A radical isn't a new thing. It's a question: what number, used this many times, builds this?

A square root is the side of a square

Drag the slider to choose a number of tiles. Can you make a perfect square out of all of them?

Aha

√36 asks "how long is the side?" 36 tiles make a 6 × 6 square, so it's 6. Most numbers (like 12) can't make a perfect square. Those are the ones we simplify instead of evaluate.

The secret: a root is a fractional exponent

Multiply x1/2 by itself. What do you get?

Aha

x1/2 is a number that gives x when multiplied by itself. That is exactly what √x means. Roots are fractional exponents, so every exponent rule still works on them. Power on top, root on the bottom: am/n.

Radicals

Pairs escape

Picture the radical sign as a house with a door that only lets out pairs (triples for a cube root). Break everything into primes and see who gets out.

72 = 2 · 2 · 2 · 3 · 3. Which is √72 in simplest form?

Aha

Why can a pair leave? Because √2 · 2 = √4 = 2: a pair is a perfect square. Pairs escape, singles stay inside. For ∛ you need three alike. Try √30 above: all primes are single, so nobody gets out.

Letters are treated as positive so that √x² = x.

The shortcut: find the biggest square hiding inside

Once you know your perfect squares, skip the breakdown. Find the largest one that divides the number.

Aha

Pick a smaller square and you still get there, just with an extra round. If the radicand still has a perfect square inside, you aren't done.

Radicals

Radicals behave like variables

Treat √3 like the letter x. Almost everything you already know about algebra carries over.

Adding: only the same kind

√2 + √3 simplifies to…

Aha

2√3 + 5√3 is like 2 apples + 5 apples. And √12 and √27 look unrelated, but both are secretly "threes". Always simplify first, then look for matches.

Multiplying and dividing: one roof

√6 · √15 = ?

Aha

Two radicals with the same index can share one roof: √a · √b = √(ab) and √a ÷ √b = √(a/b). Then break into primes and let the pairs escape.

Rationalizing: multiply by 1 in disguise

Does multiplying 3/√2 by √2/√2 change its value?

Aha

√2 · √2 = 2, so the radical vanishes from the bottom. When the bottom has two terms, use the conjugate: (a − b)(a + b) = a² − b², so the middle terms cancel and the radical goes with them.
